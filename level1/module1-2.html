Module 1.2

Second-Order Linear ODEs

Difficulty: Intermediate Estimated time: 4 – 5 hours
Prerequisites: Module 1.1 (First-Order ODEs and Modeling)

Why This Matters

Second-order linear ODEs are the mathematical backbone of oscillatory phenomena throughout science and engineering. Every time a bridge sways in the wind, a speaker cone vibrates to produce sound, or an RLC circuit filters a radio signal, a second-order ODE governs the motion. The equation \(my'' + by' + ky = F(t)\) unifies mechanical vibrations (mass–spring–damper systems), electrical circuits (inductance–resistance–capacitance networks), and wave phenomena (standing waves on a string) under a single analytical framework.

Understanding the characteristic equation and its three root cases gives you the power to predict whether a system oscillates, decays monotonically, or sits on the critical boundary between the two. The method of undetermined coefficients handles driven systems efficiently, while variation of parameters provides a universal technique when the forcing function is irregular. Most critically, the concept of resonance — when a driving frequency matches a natural frequency — explains catastrophic failures (the Tacoma Narrows Bridge) and essential technologies (MRI imaging, radio tuning) alike.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

  1. Derive the characteristic equation \(ar^2 + br + c = 0\) from a constant-coefficient second-order linear ODE \(ay'' + by' + cy = 0\) by substituting the trial solution \(y = e^{rt}\).
  2. Solve second-order homogeneous equations for all three root cases: distinct real roots, repeated real roots, and complex conjugate roots.
  3. Apply the method of undetermined coefficients to find particular solutions of \(ay'' + by' + cy = g(t)\) for polynomial, exponential, and sinusoidal forcing functions.
  4. Apply variation of parameters to find particular solutions for arbitrary continuous forcing functions, including cases where undetermined coefficients fails.
  5. Model a mass–spring–damper system as a second-order IVP \(mx'' + bx' + kx = F(t)\) and interpret physical parameters in terms of ODE coefficients.
  6. Identify resonance conditions and explain why unbounded or near-unbounded oscillations arise when the driving frequency matches the natural frequency.
  7. Compute the Wronskian \(W(y_1, y_2) = y_1 y_2' - y_1' y_2\) and use it to verify linear independence of two solutions.
  8. Classify damping regimes (underdamped, critically damped, overdamped) from the discriminant \(b^2 - 4mk\) and predict the qualitative behaviour of the solution in each case.

Core Concepts

Definitions

Definition 1.2.1 — Second-Order Linear ODE

A second-order linear ODE has the general form

$$ a(t)\,y'' + b(t)\,y' + c(t)\,y = g(t), $$

where \(a(t) \neq 0\) on the interval of interest. When the coefficients \(a, b, c\) are constants, we write \(ay'' + by' + cy = g(t)\) and call the equation a constant-coefficient second-order linear ODE. This is the most important class: it models springs, circuits, and resonators with fixed parameters.

Definition 1.2.2 — Homogeneous vs. Non-Homogeneous

The equation \(ay'' + by' + cy = g(t)\) is homogeneous when \(g(t) \equiv 0\) and non-homogeneous when \(g(t) \not\equiv 0\). The general solution of the non-homogeneous equation is

$$ y(t) = y_c(t) + y_p(t), $$

where \(y_c(t)\) is the complementary solution (general solution of the associated homogeneous equation) and \(y_p(t)\) is any particular solution of the non-homogeneous equation.

Definition 1.2.3 — Characteristic Equation

For the constant-coefficient homogeneous equation \(ay'' + by' + cy = 0\), substituting the trial solution \(y = e^{rt}\) yields the characteristic equation

$$ ar^2 + br + c = 0. $$

The roots \(r_1, r_2\) of this quadratic determine the form of the general solution. Three cases arise depending on the discriminant \(\Delta = b^2 - 4ac\):

  • Distinct real roots (\(\Delta > 0\)): \(y = c_1 e^{r_1 t} + c_2 e^{r_2 t}\).
  • Repeated real root (\(\Delta = 0\)): \(r_1 = r_2 = r\), and \(y = (c_1 + c_2 t)\,e^{rt}\).
  • Complex conjugate roots (\(\Delta < 0\)): \(r = \alpha \pm i\beta\), and \(y = e^{\alpha t}(c_1 \cos\beta t + c_2 \sin\beta t)\).

Definition 1.2.4 — Wronskian

Given two solutions \(y_1(t)\) and \(y_2(t)\) of a second-order linear ODE, their Wronskian is the determinant

$$ W(y_1, y_2)(t) = \begin{vmatrix} y_1 & y_2 \\ y_1' & y_2' \end{vmatrix} = y_1(t)\,y_2'(t) - y_1'(t)\,y_2(t). $$

The two solutions form a fundamental set (i.e., are linearly independent) on an interval \(I\) if and only if \(W(y_1, y_2)(t) \neq 0\) for some (and hence every) \(t \in I\).

Definition 1.2.5 — Resonance

Consider the undamped driven oscillator \(my'' + ky = F_0 \cos(\omega t)\) with natural frequency \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}\). Resonance occurs when the driving frequency \(\omega\) equals the natural frequency \(\omega_0\). In this case the standard method of undetermined coefficients fails (the trial solution is already part of the complementary solution), and the particular solution takes the form

$$ y_p(t) = \frac{F_0}{2m\omega_0}\,t\sin(\omega_0 t), $$

which grows without bound as \(t \to \infty\). Physically, the external force continuously pumps energy into the system at exactly the frequency at which it oscillates most readily.

Definition 1.2.6 — Fundamental Set of Solutions

A pair of solutions \(\{y_1, y_2\}\) of the homogeneous equation \(ay'' + by' + cy = 0\) is called a fundamental set of solutions if every solution \(y(t)\) can be written as a linear combination \(y = c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2\) for appropriate constants \(c_1, c_2\). Equivalently, \(\{y_1, y_2\}\) is a fundamental set if and only if \(W(y_1, y_2) \neq 0\).

Theorems

Theorem 1.2.1 — Superposition Principle

If \(y_1(t)\) and \(y_2(t)\) are solutions of the homogeneous linear equation \(ay'' + by' + cy = 0\), then for any constants \(c_1, c_2\), the linear combination

$$ y(t) = c_1\,y_1(t) + c_2\,y_2(t) $$

is also a solution. More generally, if \(y_{p_1}\) is a particular solution of \(ay'' + by' + cy = g_1(t)\) and \(y_{p_2}\) is a particular solution of \(ay'' + by' + cy = g_2(t)\), then \(y_{p_1} + y_{p_2}\) is a particular solution of \(ay'' + by' + cy = g_1(t) + g_2(t)\).

Proof sketch. Linearity of the differential operator \(L[y] = ay'' + by' + cy\) gives \(L[c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2] = c_1 L[y_1] + c_2 L[y_2] = 0\).

Theorem 1.2.2 — Abel's Theorem on the Wronskian

Let \(y_1, y_2\) be solutions of \(y'' + p(t)\,y' + q(t)\,y = 0\) on an interval \(I\) where \(p\) and \(q\) are continuous. Then the Wronskian satisfies

$$ W(y_1, y_2)(t) = W(y_1, y_2)(t_0)\,\exp\!\left(-\int_{t_0}^{t} p(s)\,ds\right) $$

for any \(t_0 \in I\). In particular, \(W\) is either identically zero or never zero on \(I\).

Proof sketch. Differentiating the Wronskian and using the ODE to eliminate \(y_1''\) and \(y_2''\), one obtains the first-order ODE \(W' = -p(t)\,W\), whose solution is the exponential formula above.

Consequence: To check linear independence of two solutions, it suffices to evaluate \(W\) at a single convenient point. If \(W(t_0) \neq 0\), the solutions form a fundamental set.

Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1: Confusing particular and complementary solutions

Error: Students frequently write the general solution as just \(y_p(t)\) or just \(y_c(t)\), forgetting that the complete solution of a non-homogeneous equation requires both parts: \(y = y_c + y_p\).

Correction: The complementary solution \(y_c\) captures all solutions of the homogeneous equation (it contains the arbitrary constants); the particular solution \(y_p\) accounts for the forcing function \(g(t)\). Neither alone is the general solution. Always write \(y = y_c + y_p\).

Misconception 2: Applying undetermined coefficients when the guess is already in \(y_c\)

Error: When solving \(y'' + 4y = \cos(2t)\), students guess \(y_p = A\cos(2t) + B\sin(2t)\) and find the system for \(A, B\) is inconsistent.

Correction: Because \(\cos(2t)\) and \(\sin(2t)\) are already solutions of the homogeneous equation, the guess must be modified by multiplying by \(t\): try \(y_p = t(A\cos 2t + B\sin 2t)\). This is the "modification rule" for undetermined coefficients.

Misconception 3: Believing the Wronskian can be zero at some points and nonzero at others

Error: Students sometimes think \(W(t)\) might vanish at isolated points while being nonzero elsewhere.

Correction: Abel's theorem shows that for solutions of a linear ODE, the Wronskian is either identically zero or never zero on the entire interval. There is no in-between. If \(W(t_0) \neq 0\) at one point, then \(W(t) \neq 0\) everywhere.

Misconception 4: Thinking "critically damped" means no motion at all

Error: Students interpret critical damping as the system being frozen in place or as infinite damping.

Correction: Critical damping (\(b^2 = 4mk\)) means the system returns to equilibrium as fast as possible without oscillating. The solution \(y = (c_1 + c_2 t)e^{-bt/(2m)}\) still moves — it just decays monotonically instead of oscillating. It is the boundary between oscillatory (underdamped) and non-oscillatory (overdamped) behaviour.

Misconception 5: Resonance requires zero damping

Error: Students believe resonance can only occur in the purely undamped case \(b = 0\).

Correction: While pure resonance (unbounded growth) requires zero damping, practical resonance occurs in damped systems when the driving frequency \(\omega\) is near the natural frequency \(\omega_0\). The amplitude of the steady-state response peaks at a frequency \(\omega_r = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - b^2/(2m^2)}\) (slightly below \(\omega_0\)), and the peak amplitude can be very large when damping is small.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — Constant-Coefficient IVP with Distinct Real Roots

Problem. Solve the initial-value problem

$$ y'' + 5y' + 6y = 0, \qquad y(0) = 2,\quad y'(0) = 3. $$

Step 1 — Characteristic equation. Substituting \(y = e^{rt}\) gives

$$ r^2 + 5r + 6 = 0. $$

Factoring: \((r + 2)(r + 3) = 0\), so \(r_1 = -2\) and \(r_2 = -3\). These are distinct real roots.

Step 2 — General solution. By the distinct-real-roots formula:

$$ y(t) = c_1 e^{-2t} + c_2 e^{-3t}. $$

Step 3 — Verify linear independence. The Wronskian is

$$ W(e^{-2t}, e^{-3t}) = \begin{vmatrix} e^{-2t} & e^{-3t} \\ -2e^{-2t} & -3e^{-3t} \end{vmatrix} = -3e^{-5t} + 2e^{-5t} = -e^{-5t} \neq 0. $$

The solutions are linearly independent, confirming a fundamental set.

Step 4 — Apply initial conditions. We need \(y'(t) = -2c_1 e^{-2t} - 3c_2 e^{-3t}\). At \(t = 0\):

$$ y(0) = c_1 + c_2 = 2, $$ $$ y'(0) = -2c_1 - 3c_2 = 3. $$

From the first equation, \(c_2 = 2 - c_1\). Substituting into the second:

$$ -2c_1 - 3(2 - c_1) = 3 \;\Longrightarrow\; -2c_1 - 6 + 3c_1 = 3 \;\Longrightarrow\; c_1 = 9. $$

Then \(c_2 = 2 - 9 = -7\).

Solution:

$$ \boxed{y(t) = 9e^{-2t} - 7e^{-3t}.} $$

Verification. As \(t \to \infty\), both terms decay to zero, consistent with both roots being negative (stable node). At \(t = 0\): \(y(0) = 9 - 7 = 2\) and \(y'(0) = -18 + 21 = 3\). The initial conditions are satisfied.

Example 2 — Resonance and Variation of Parameters

Problem. Solve

$$ y'' + 4y = 3\sin(2t). $$

Step 1 — Identify resonance. The homogeneous equation \(y'' + 4y = 0\) has characteristic equation \(r^2 + 4 = 0\), giving \(r = \pm 2i\). The complementary solution is

$$ y_c(t) = c_1 \cos(2t) + c_2 \sin(2t). $$

The forcing function \(g(t) = 3\sin(2t)\) has frequency \(\omega = 2\), which equals the natural frequency \(\omega_0 = 2\). This is resonance: the standard undetermined-coefficients guess \(A\cos(2t) + B\sin(2t)\) is already in \(y_c\), so we must use variation of parameters (or the modification rule).

Step 2 — Set up variation of parameters. Write the equation in standard form \(y'' + 4y = 3\sin(2t)\) (already in standard form with \(p = 0\), \(q = 4\)). We have \(y_1 = \cos(2t)\), \(y_2 = \sin(2t)\). The Wronskian is:

$$ W = \begin{vmatrix} \cos 2t & \sin 2t \\ -2\sin 2t & 2\cos 2t \end{vmatrix} = 2\cos^2(2t) + 2\sin^2(2t) = 2. $$

Step 3 — Compute \(u_1'\) and \(u_2'\). With \(g(t) = 3\sin(2t)\):

$$ u_1'(t) = -\frac{y_2\,g}{W} = -\frac{\sin(2t) \cdot 3\sin(2t)}{2} = -\frac{3}{2}\sin^2(2t) = -\frac{3}{4}\bigl(1 - \cos(4t)\bigr), $$ $$ u_2'(t) = \frac{y_1\,g}{W} = \frac{\cos(2t) \cdot 3\sin(2t)}{2} = \frac{3}{4}\sin(4t). $$

Step 4 — Integrate.

$$ u_1(t) = -\frac{3}{4}\left(t - \frac{\sin(4t)}{4}\right) = -\frac{3t}{4} + \frac{3\sin(4t)}{16}, $$ $$ u_2(t) = \frac{3}{4}\left(-\frac{\cos(4t)}{4}\right) = -\frac{3\cos(4t)}{16}. $$

Step 5 — Particular solution.

$$ y_p = u_1 y_1 + u_2 y_2 = \left(-\frac{3t}{4} + \frac{3\sin 4t}{16}\right)\cos 2t + \left(-\frac{3\cos 4t}{16}\right)\sin 2t. $$

Expanding: use the identities \(\sin(4t)\cos(2t) - \cos(4t)\sin(2t) = \sin(4t - 2t) = \sin(2t)\):

$$ y_p = -\frac{3t}{4}\cos(2t) + \frac{3}{16}\sin(2t). $$

The \(\sin(2t)\) term can be absorbed into \(y_c\), so the essential particular solution is:

$$ y_p(t) = -\frac{3t}{4}\cos(2t). $$

General solution:

$$ \boxed{y(t) = c_1\cos(2t) + c_2\sin(2t) - \frac{3t}{4}\cos(2t).} $$

Observation. The \(t\cos(2t)\) term grows linearly in amplitude — the hallmark of resonance. The system absorbs energy from the driving force without bound, confirming that \(\omega = \omega_0 = 2\) is the resonance frequency.

Agent Lens

Vibration Control Agent

Consider an intelligent agent tasked with controlling vibrations in a mass–spring–damper system. The agent continuously monitors the system's state \(\mathbf{s} = (x, \dot{x})\) — position and velocity — and must decide how to adjust the damping coefficient \(b\) in real time to minimise the total mechanical energy

$$ E(t) = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{x}^2 + \frac{1}{2}kx^2 $$

as quickly as possible without causing unwanted oscillations.

Agent design:

  • State space: \(\mathcal{S} = \{(x, \dot{x}) \in \mathbb{R}^2\}\). The agent observes both position and velocity at each time step.
  • Action space: \(\mathcal{A} = \{b \in [0, b_{\max}]\}\). At each step, the agent selects a damping coefficient. This could model an adjustable magnetorheological damper in a vehicle suspension.
  • Reward signal: \(r_t = -E(t)\). The agent is penalised proportionally to the remaining energy, incentivising rapid energy dissipation.
  • Transition dynamics: Given action \(b_t\), the state evolves according to \(m\ddot{x} + b_t \dot{x} + kx = 0\) for one time step.

Optimal strategy from ODE theory:

  • The rate of energy dissipation is \(\frac{dE}{dt} = -b\dot{x}^2 \leq 0\). Energy is dissipated only when the mass is moving (\(\dot{x} \neq 0\)) and damping is positive.
  • When \(|\dot{x}|\) is large, the agent should apply maximum damping (\(b = b_{\max}\)) to extract energy rapidly.
  • When the mass passes through equilibrium (\(x \approx 0\), \(|\dot{x}|\) is maximal), damping is most effective. When the mass is at maximum displacement (\(\dot{x} \approx 0\)), damping has no effect.
  • If the agent can achieve critical damping \(b = 2\sqrt{mk}\), it guarantees the fastest non-oscillatory return to equilibrium. This is the optimal policy in the class of constant-damping strategies.
  • A time-varying policy \(b(t)\) can outperform any constant damping by applying high damping when \(|\dot{x}|\) is large and reducing it when \(|\dot{x}|\) is small, a bang–bang-like strategy.

Connection to reinforcement learning: This vibration control problem is a continuous-state, continuous-action control task. The analytical results from ODE theory (energy dissipation rate, critical damping formula, damping regimes) provide the ground truth against which a learned RL policy can be benchmarked. An RL agent trained without knowledge of the ODE structure should rediscover that (a) damping should be large when velocity is high, and (b) the critical damping threshold separates oscillatory from non-oscillatory behaviour.

Homogeneous: \(r^2 + 3r + 2 = (r+1)(r+2) = 0\), so \(r_1 = -1\), \(r_2 = -2\). Thus \(y_c = c_1 e^{-t} + c_2 e^{-2t}\).

Exercise 5. Write a Python function that takes coefficients \(a, b, c\) of \(ay'' + by' + cy = 0\) and returns the general solution type ("distinct real", "repeated real", or "complex conjugate") along with the roots. Test on five different equations.

import numpy as np

def classify_char_eq(a, b, c):
    disc = b**2 - 4*a*c
    if disc > 1e-12:
        r1 = (-b + np.sqrt(disc)) / (2*a)
        r2 = (-b - np.sqrt(disc)) / (2*a)
        return "distinct real", (r1, r2)
    elif abs(disc) < 1e-12:
        r = -b / (2*a)
        return "repeated real", (r,)
    else:
        alpha = -b / (2*a)
        beta = np.sqrt(-disc) / (2*a)
        return "complex conjugate", (alpha, beta)

# Tests
tests = [(1,5,6), (1,-4,4), (1,0,9), (1,2,5), (1,-6,9)]
for a, b, c in tests:
    kind, roots = classify_char_eq(a, b, c)
    print(f"{a}r^2 + {b}r + {c} = 0: {kind}, roots = {roots}")

Exercise 6. Numerically solve \(y'' + 0.5y' + 4y = 0\), \(y(0) = 2\), \(y'(0) = 0\) using both Euler's method and RK4 with step sizes \(\Delta t = 0.1, 0.01, 0.001\). Plot the error \(|y_{\text{numerical}} - y_{\text{exact}}|\) at \(t = 10\) versus step size on a log–log plot. Verify first-order and fourth-order convergence.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Exact solution for y'' + 0.5y' + 4y = 0, y(0)=2, y'(0)=0
# Characteristic: r^2 + 0.5r + 4 = 0
# r = -0.25 +/- i*sqrt(4 - 0.0625) = -0.25 +/- i*1.9843
alpha = -0.25
beta = np.sqrt(4 - 0.0625)

def exact(t):
    # y(0) = c1 = 2;  y'(0) = alpha*c1 + beta*c2 = 0 => c2 = -alpha*c1/beta
    c1 = 2.0
    c2 = -alpha * c1 / beta
    return np.exp(alpha * t) * (c1 * np.cos(beta * t) + c2 * np.sin(beta * t))

def solve_euler(dt, t_end=10.0):
    N = int(t_end / dt)
    x, v = 2.0, 0.0
    for _ in range(N):
        x_new = x + dt * v
        v_new = v + dt * (-0.5 * v - 4 * x)
        x, v = x_new, v_new
    return x

def solve_rk4(dt, t_end=10.0):
    N = int(t_end / dt)
    x, v = 2.0, 0.0
    def f(x, v):
        return v, -0.5*v - 4*x
    for _ in range(N):
        k1x, k1v = f(x, v)
        k2x, k2v = f(x + 0.5*dt*k1x, v + 0.5*dt*k1v)
        k3x, k3v = f(x + 0.5*dt*k2x, v + 0.5*dt*k2v)
        k4x, k4v = f(x + dt*k3x, v + dt*k3v)
        x += (dt/6)*(k1x + 2*k2x + 2*k3x + k4x)
        v += (dt/6)*(k1v + 2*k2v + 2*k3v + k4v)
    return x

y_true = exact(10.0)
dts = [0.1, 0.01, 0.001]
euler_err = [abs(solve_euler(dt) - y_true) for dt in dts]
rk4_err = [abs(solve_rk4(dt) - y_true) for dt in dts]

plt.loglog(dts, euler_err, 'o-', label='Euler (order 1)')
plt.loglog(dts, rk4_err, 's-', label='RK4 (order 4)')
plt.xlabel('Step size dt')
plt.ylabel('|Error| at t=10')
plt.legend()
plt.grid(True)
plt.title('Convergence comparison')
plt.show()

import numpy as np

def wronskian_numerical(f1, f2, t, h=1e-6):
    f1_val = f1(t)
    f2_val = f2(t)
    f1_prime = (f1(t + h) - f1(t - h)) / (2 * h)
    f2_prime = (f2(t + h) - f2(t - h)) / (2 * h)
    return f1_val * f2_prime - f1_prime * f2_val

    print(f"W at t={t_test:.3f}: {W:.10f}")

Exercise 9 (Agentic). Design a vibration control agent for the system \(m x'' + b(t) x' + k x = 0\) with \(m = 1\), \(k = 4\), and the agent can choose \(b(t) \in [0, 10]\) at each time step. The initial condition is \(x(0) = 5\), \(\dot{x}(0) = 0\). Implement a simple policy: \(b(t) = b_{\max}\) when \(|\dot{x}| > \epsilon\) and \(b(t) = 0\) otherwise. Compare the energy decay \(E(t) = \frac{1}{2}\dot{x}^2 + 2x^2\) against a constant critically damped policy \(b = 2\sqrt{mk} = 4\). Which dissipates energy faster?

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

m, k = 1.0, 4.0
x0, v0 = 5.0, 0.0
b_max = 10.0
eps = 0.01
dt = 0.005
T = 10.0
N = int(T / dt)

def simulate(b_func):
    x, v = x0, v0
    energies = []
    for i in range(N):
        E = 0.5 * m * v**2 + 0.5 * k * x**2
        energies.append(E)
        b = b_func(x, v)
        # RK4 step
        def f(x_, v_):
            return v_, -(b/m)*v_ - (k/m)*x_
        k1x, k1v = f(x, v)
        k2x, k2v = f(x+0.5*dt*k1x, v+0.5*dt*k1v)
        k3x, k3v = f(x+0.5*dt*k2x, v+0.5*dt*k2v)
        k4x, k4v = f(x+dt*k3x, v+dt*k3v)
        x += (dt/6)*(k1x+2*k2x+2*k3x+k4x)
        v += (dt/6)*(k1v+2*k2v+2*k3v+k4v)
    return np.array(energies)

# Policy 1: bang-bang agent
E_agent = simulate(lambda x, v: b_max if abs(v) > eps else 0)

# Policy 2: constant critical damping
b_crit = 2 * np.sqrt(m * k)
E_crit = simulate(lambda x, v: b_crit)

t = np.linspace(0, T, N)
plt.semilogy(t, E_agent, label=f'Agent (bang-bang, b_max={b_max})')
plt.semilogy(t, E_crit, label=f'Critical damping (b={b_crit})')
plt.xlabel('Time'); plt.ylabel('Energy E(t)')
plt.legend(); plt.title('Energy dissipation comparison')
plt.show()

The bang-bang agent typically dissipates energy faster than constant critical damping because it applies maximum damping precisely when velocity (and hence dissipation rate \(b\dot{x}^2\)) is highest.

Assessment

Quiz (10 Questions)

Q1. The characteristic equation of \(2y'' + 5y' + 3y = 0\) is:

(a) \(2r^2 + 5r + 3 = 0\)   (b) \(r^2 + 5r + 3 = 0\)   (c) \(2r^2 - 5r + 3 = 0\)   (d) \(r^2 + 5r + 6 = 0\)

Answer: (a). Substituting \(y = e^{rt}\) into \(2y'' + 5y' + 3y = 0\) yields \((2r^2 + 5r + 3)e^{rt} = 0\).

Q2. If the characteristic equation has roots \(r = 3 \pm 4i\), the general solution of the homogeneous equation is:

(a) \(c_1 e^{3t}\cos 4t + c_2 e^{3t}\sin 4t\)   (b) \(c_1 e^{4t}\cos 3t + c_2 e^{4t}\sin 3t\)   (c) \((c_1 + c_2 t)e^{3t}\)   (d) \(c_1 e^{3t} + c_2 e^{4t}\)

Answer: (a). For complex roots \(\alpha \pm i\beta\), the solution is \(e^{\alpha t}(c_1 \cos\beta t + c_2 \sin\beta t)\) with \(\alpha = 3\), \(\beta = 4\).

Q3. The Wronskian of \(e^{2t}\) and \(e^{5t}\) is:

(a) \(3e^{7t}\)   (b) \(7e^{7t}\)   (c) \(-3e^{7t}\)   (d) \(e^{7t}\)

Answer: (a). \(W = e^{2t} \cdot 5e^{5t} - 2e^{2t} \cdot e^{5t} = 5e^{7t} - 2e^{7t} = 3e^{7t}\).

Q4. A mass–spring system with \(m = 2\), \(b = 8\), \(k = 8\) is:

(a) Underdamped   (b) Critically damped   (c) Overdamped   (d) Undamped

Answer: (b). The discriminant is \(b^2 - 4mk = 64 - 64 = 0\). Zero discriminant means critical damping.

Q5. For the equation \(y'' + \omega_0^2 y = F_0\cos(\omega t)\), resonance occurs when:

(a) \(\omega = 0\)   (b) \(\omega = 2\omega_0\)   (c) \(\omega = \omega_0\)   (d) \(\omega = \omega_0/2\)

Answer: (c). Resonance occurs when the driving frequency matches the natural frequency.

Q6. In variation of parameters for \(y'' + p(t)y' + q(t)y = g(t)\) with fundamental solutions \(y_1, y_2\), the formula for \(u_2'\) is:

(a) \(\frac{y_1 g}{W}\)   (b) \(-\frac{y_1 g}{W}\)   (c) \(\frac{y_2 g}{W}\)   (d) \(-\frac{y_2 g}{W}\)

Answer: (a). \(u_2' = \frac{y_1 g}{W}\) and \(u_1' = -\frac{y_2 g}{W}\).

Q7. The general solution of \(y'' - 6y' + 9y = 0\) is:

(a) \(c_1 e^{3t} + c_2 e^{-3t}\)   (b) \((c_1 + c_2 t)e^{3t}\)   (c) \(c_1\cos 3t + c_2\sin 3t\)   (d) \(c_1 e^{3t} + c_2 te^{-3t}\)

Answer: (b). The characteristic equation \(r^2 - 6r + 9 = (r-3)^2 = 0\) has a repeated root \(r = 3\).

Q8. When using undetermined coefficients for \(y'' + 4y' + 4y = e^{-2t}\), the correct trial particular solution is:

(a) \(Ae^{-2t}\)   (b) \(Ate^{-2t}\)   (c) \(At^2 e^{-2t}\)   (d) \(A(t+1)e^{-2t}\)

Answer: (c). The characteristic equation has \(r = -2\) as a repeated root, so both \(e^{-2t}\) and \(te^{-2t}\) are in \(y_c\). Multiply by \(t^2\).

Q9. Abel's theorem states that the Wronskian of two solutions of \(y'' + p(t)y' + q(t)y = 0\) satisfies:

(a) \(W' = p(t)W\)   (b) \(W' = -p(t)W\)   (c) \(W' = q(t)W\)   (d) \(W' = 0\)

Answer: (b). Abel's theorem gives \(W' = -p(t)W\), hence \(W(t) = W(t_0)\exp(-\int_{t_0}^{t} p(s)\,ds)\).

Q10. A vibration control agent should apply maximum damping when:

(a) Displacement is maximum   (b) Velocity is maximum   (c) Energy is zero   (d) The system is at rest

Answer: (b). The energy dissipation rate is \(dE/dt = -b\dot{x}^2\), which is largest when \(|\dot{x}|\) is maximum.
