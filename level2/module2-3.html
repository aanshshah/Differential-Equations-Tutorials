Module 2.3

Stochastic and Complex Models

Difficulty: Advanced
Estimated Time: 4 -- 5 hours
Prerequisites:
  • Module 2.1 — Scalar PDEs and Classification
  • Basic probability (random variables, expectation, variance, normal distribution)

Why This Matters

Deterministic PDEs describe idealised systems. Real-world systems are buffeted by noise: thermal fluctuations drive Brownian motion, market prices exhibit random volatility, and biological systems are subject to stochastic gene expression. Stochastic differential equations (SDEs) extend the ODE/PDE framework to incorporate randomness in a mathematically rigorous way through Ito calculus. The celebrated Black-Scholes equation, which revolutionised financial derivatives pricing, is derived by applying Ito's lemma to a geometric Brownian motion model of stock prices. The Euler-Maruyama method gives a practical numerical scheme for simulating SDE paths, and Monte Carlo methods turn those paths into statistical estimates of quantities like option prices or mean exit times. Beyond finance, coupled reaction-diffusion systems with stochastic perturbations produce Turing patterns that explain biological morphogenesis. This module bridges the deterministic PDE world of Module 2.1 with the probabilistic reality of complex systems.

Core Concepts

Key Definitions

Definition 2.3.1 — Brownian Motion (Wiener Process)

A standard Brownian motion (or Wiener process) \(W(t)\), \(t\geq 0\), is a stochastic process satisfying:

  1. \(W(0) = 0\) almost surely.
  2. For \(0\leq s < t\), the increment \(W(t)-W(s)\) is normally distributed with mean 0 and variance \(t-s\).
  3. Increments on non-overlapping intervals are independent.
  4. The sample paths \(t\mapsto W(t)\) are continuous almost surely.

A crucial consequence: Brownian motion paths are nowhere differentiable with probability 1, so the classical chain rule fails and must be replaced by Ito's lemma.

Definition 2.3.2 — Ito Stochastic Differential Equation

An Ito SDE is written in differential form as

$$ dX_t = \mu(X_t, t)\,dt + \sigma(X_t, t)\,dW_t, $$

where \(\mu\) is the drift coefficient and \(\sigma\) is the diffusion coefficient. The rigorous meaning is the integral equation

$$ X_t = X_0 + \int_0^t \mu(X_s,s)\,ds + \int_0^t \sigma(X_s,s)\,dW_s, $$

where the second integral is the Ito integral.

Definition 2.3.3 — Geometric Brownian Motion

Geometric Brownian motion (GBM) is the solution of the SDE

$$ dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t, $$

where \(\mu\) (drift) and \(\sigma\) (volatility) are constants. By Ito's lemma applied to \(\ln S_t\), the exact solution is

$$ S_t = S_0 \exp\!\left[\left(\mu - \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)t + \sigma W_t\right]. $$

GBM is the standard model for stock prices in the Black-Scholes framework.

Definition 2.3.4 — Euler-Maruyama Method

The Euler-Maruyama method discretises an Ito SDE on a time grid \(t_n = n\Delta t\):

$$ X_{n+1} = X_n + \mu(X_n, t_n)\,\Delta t + \sigma(X_n, t_n)\,\Delta W_n, $$

where \(\Delta W_n = W(t_{n+1})-W(t_n) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Delta t)\). In practice, each \(\Delta W_n\) is generated as \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_n\) where \(Z_n\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\).

Definition 2.3.5 — Strong and Weak Convergence

A numerical SDE method has strong order of convergence \(\gamma\) if

$$ \mathbb{E}\bigl[|X_N - X(T)|\bigr] \leq C\,(\Delta t)^{\gamma}, $$

and weak order of convergence \(\beta\) if for every sufficiently smooth function \(g\),

$$ \bigl|\mathbb{E}[g(X_N)] - \mathbb{E}[g(X(T))]\bigr| \leq C\,(\Delta t)^{\beta}. $$

Euler-Maruyama has strong order \(\gamma=0.5\) and weak order \(\beta=1\).

Definition 2.3.6 — Reaction-Diffusion System

A reaction-diffusion system couples two (or more) species \(u\) and \(v\):

$$ \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = D_u \nabla^2 u + f(u,v), \qquad \frac{\partial v}{\partial t} = D_v \nabla^2 v + g(u,v), $$

where \(D_u, D_v\) are diffusion coefficients and \(f,g\) describe the reaction kinetics. When \(D_v \gg D_u\) and the reaction kinetics satisfy certain conditions, a Turing instability can arise, producing spontaneous spatial patterns from an initially homogeneous state.

Key Theorems

Theorem 2.3.1 — Ito's Lemma

Let \(X_t\) satisfy the Ito SDE \(dX_t = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t\) and let \(f(x,t)\in C^{2,1}\). Then the process \(Y_t = f(X_t, t)\) satisfies

$$ dY_t = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \mu\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}\right)dt + \sigma\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,dW_t. $$

The extra term \(\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}\,dt\) is the Ito correction, absent in the deterministic chain rule, and arises because \((dW_t)^2 = dt\) in the Ito calculus.

Proof sketch. Taylor-expand \(f(X_t+dX_t, t+dt)\) to second order. The key is that \((dW_t)^2\) has expectation \(dt\) and variance \(2(dt)^2\), so in the \(dt\to 0\) limit, \((dW_t)^2 \to dt\) in mean square. The cross terms \(dt\cdot dW_t\) and \((dt)^2\) are of higher order and vanish. Collecting the \(dt\) and \(dW_t\) terms gives the stated formula.

Theorem 2.3.2 — Convergence of Euler-Maruyama

Suppose the drift \(\mu\) and diffusion \(\sigma\) satisfy global Lipschitz and linear growth conditions. Then the Euler-Maruyama approximation \(X_N\) at \(T=N\Delta t\) satisfies:

  • Strong convergence: \(\mathbb{E}[|X_N - X(T)|] = \mathcal{O}((\Delta t)^{1/2})\).
  • Weak convergence: \(|\mathbb{E}[g(X_N)] - \mathbb{E}[g(X(T))]| = \mathcal{O}(\Delta t)\) for smooth \(g\).

The strong order \(1/2\) means that pathwise accuracy improves slowly with refinement, while the weak order 1 means that expectations converge at the same rate as the deterministic Euler method.

Proof sketch (strong convergence). Define the error \(e_n = X_n - X(t_n)\). Using the Ito integral form and the Lipschitz condition, one obtains \(\mathbb{E}[|e_{n+1}|^2] \leq (1+C\Delta t)\mathbb{E}[|e_n|^2] + C'(\Delta t)^2\). A discrete Gronwall inequality gives \(\mathbb{E}[|e_N|^2]\leq K\,\Delta t\), hence \(\mathbb{E}[|e_N|]=\mathcal{O}((\Delta t)^{1/2})\).

Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1: "Brownian motion paths are smooth since they are continuous."

Continuity does not imply differentiability. Brownian paths are continuous but nowhere differentiable with probability 1. Their total variation on any interval is infinite, and they have Holder exponent \(1/2-\epsilon\) but not \(1/2\). This irregularity is precisely what necessitates the Ito integral.

Misconception 2: "The ordinary chain rule applies to functions of Brownian motion."

Because \((dW_t)^2 = dt \neq 0\), the second-order term in the Taylor expansion contributes at leading order. Ito's lemma includes the correction \(\frac{\sigma^2}{2}f_{xx}\,dt\). Ignoring this term (using the ordinary chain rule) gives the wrong drift and leads to incorrect SDE solutions.

Misconception 3: "More Monte Carlo paths always improve accuracy proportionally."

The Monte Carlo standard error decreases as \(1/\sqrt{N}\) where \(N\) is the number of paths. To halve the error you need four times as many paths. Variance reduction techniques (antithetic variates, control variates, importance sampling) can improve efficiency far more than brute-force path generation.

Misconception 4: "The Black-Scholes formula always gives correct option prices."

Black-Scholes assumes constant volatility, continuous trading, no transaction costs, and log-normal stock prices. In reality, volatility varies (the "volatility smile"), markets are discrete, and extreme events (fat tails) are far more common than the Gaussian model predicts. The formula is a first approximation, not ground truth.

Misconception 5: "Turing patterns require stochasticity."

The classical Turing instability is a purely deterministic phenomenon arising from the interplay of short-range activation and long-range inhibition. Stochastic noise can trigger pattern formation from a spatially homogeneous state (by providing the initial perturbation), but the pattern-forming mechanism itself is the deterministic instability of the uniform steady state.

Worked Examples

Example 2.3.1 — Deriving the Black-Scholes PDE

Problem. Derive the Black-Scholes PDE for the price \(V(S,t)\) of a European option on a stock following geometric Brownian motion \(dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW\).

Step 1 — Apply Ito's lemma to \(V(S,t)\).

$$ dV = \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \mu S\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} + \frac{\sigma^2 S^2}{2}\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}\right)dt + \sigma S\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\,dW. $$

Step 2 — Construct a hedged portfolio. Consider a portfolio \(\Pi = V - \Delta \cdot S\) where \(\Delta = \partial V/\partial S\). Then

$$ d\Pi = dV - \Delta\,dS = \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \frac{\sigma^2 S^2}{2}\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}\right)dt. $$

The \(dW\) terms cancel, so the portfolio is riskless over the infinitesimal interval \(dt\).

Step 3 — No-arbitrage condition. A riskless portfolio must earn the risk-free rate \(r\):

$$ d\Pi = r\,\Pi\,dt = r\left(V - S\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\right)dt. $$

Step 4 — Equate and simplify:

$$ \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \frac{\sigma^2 S^2}{2}\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} = r\,V - r\,S\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}. $$

The Black-Scholes PDE:

$$ \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + r\,S\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} + \frac{\sigma^2 S^2}{2}\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} - r\,V = 0. $$

This is a backward parabolic PDE (time runs backward from the expiry date). The drift \(\mu\) does not appear; only the risk-free rate \(r\) and volatility \(\sigma\) matter. This is the principle of risk-neutral pricing.

Example 2.3.2 — Solving the Geometric Brownian Motion SDE

Problem. Find the exact solution of \(dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t\) with \(S_0 > 0\).

Step 1 — Let \(Y_t = \ln S_t\). Apply Ito's lemma with \(f(s) = \ln s\):

$$ f'(s) = \frac{1}{s}, \qquad f''(s) = -\frac{1}{s^2}. $$

Step 2 — Compute \(dY_t\):

$$ dY_t = \frac{1}{S_t}\bigl(\mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t\bigr) + \frac{1}{2}\left(-\frac{1}{S_t^2}\right)(\sigma S_t)^2\,dt $$ $$ = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\,dt = \left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)dt + \sigma\,dW_t. $$

Step 3 — Integrate:

$$ Y_t - Y_0 = \left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)t + \sigma W_t. $$

Step 4 — Exponentiate:

$$ S_t = S_0\exp\!\left[\left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)t + \sigma W_t\right]. $$

The term \(-\sigma^2/2\) is the Ito correction. Without it (i.e., using the ordinary chain rule), one would incorrectly get \(\mathbb{E}[S_t] = S_0 e^{(\mu+\sigma^2/2)t}\) instead of the correct \(\mathbb{E}[S_t]=S_0 e^{\mu t}\).

Example 2.3.3 — Monte Carlo Estimation of a European Call Option Price

Problem. Estimate the price of a European call option with strike \(K=100\), expiry \(T=1\), on a stock with \(S_0=100\), \(\mu=0.05\), \(\sigma=0.2\), at risk-free rate \(r=0.03\).

Step 1 — Under the risk-neutral measure, the stock follows \(dS = rS\,dt + \sigma S\,dW\), so

$$ S_T = S_0\exp\!\left[\left(r-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)T + \sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z\right], \quad Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1). $$

Step 2 — The option price is the discounted expected payoff:

$$ V_0 = e^{-rT}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[\max(S_T - K,\,0)\bigr]. $$

Step 3 — Monte Carlo: generate \(N\) independent samples \(Z_1,\ldots,Z_N\), compute \(S_T^{(i)}\) for each, and estimate

$$ \hat{V}_0 = e^{-rT}\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \max\!\left(S_T^{(i)}-K,\,0\right). $$

Step 4 — The standard error is \(\text{SE}=\hat{\sigma}_{\text{payoff}}/\sqrt{N}\), where \(\hat{\sigma}_{\text{payoff}}\) is the sample standard deviation of the discounted payoffs. With \(N=100{,}000\) the SE is typically below 0.05, giving a reliable estimate.

The Black-Scholes closed-form price for comparison is approximately \(V_0 \approx 7.96\) (obtained via the cumulative normal distribution formula).

Agent Lens: Monte Carlo Sampling Agent

We frame Monte Carlo estimation as a sequential decision problem. The agent observes the evolving estimate and its variance, and must decide how to allocate the remaining computational budget to minimise estimation error.

State

At step \(k\) (having generated \(k\) samples so far), the agent observes:

  • The current Monte Carlo estimate \(\hat{\mu}_k = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^k f(X_T^{(i)})\).
  • The running sample variance \(\hat{\sigma}_k^2\).
  • The current standard error \(\text{SE}_k = \hat{\sigma}_k / \sqrt{k}\).
  • The remaining computational budget (e.g., maximum paths \(N_{\max} - k\)).
  • The target precision \(\epsilon\) specified by the user.

Action

The agent selects from:

  • Generate more paths: choose a batch size \(b \in \{10, 100, 1000, 10000\}\) for the next round.
  • Switch variance-reduction strategy: toggle between plain MC, antithetic variates, or control variates.
  • Refine time discretisation: halve \(\Delta t\) for the Euler-Maruyama simulation (improving path accuracy at the cost of more computation per path).
  • Stop: declare the current estimate as final if \(\text{SE}_k < \epsilon\).

Reward

  • \(+10\) if the final estimate is within \(\epsilon\) of the true value (or a high-accuracy reference).
  • \(-\text{cost}\) proportional to the total number of Euler-Maruyama steps computed across all paths.
  • \(+5\) bonus for stopping early (using fewer than \(N_{\max}/2\) paths) while still meeting the precision target.
  • \(-20\) if the agent exhausts the budget without achieving \(\text{SE}_k < \epsilon\).

Learning

Through episodes with varying \(\sigma\), \(T\), and payoff functions, the agent learns that high-variance problems benefit from switching to antithetic variates early, while low-variance problems can achieve the target with plain MC and larger batches. It discovers that refining \(\Delta t\) is only worthwhile when the discretisation bias (weak error) dominates the statistical error, and that there is an optimal trade-off between path count and path accuracy. The converged policy resembles the adaptive Monte Carlo strategies used in production financial computing, where the sample budget is allocated dynamically based on running diagnostics.

Analytical Exercises

Exercise 2.3.1. Use Ito's lemma to find \(d(W_t^2)\) where \(W_t\) is a standard Brownian motion. Verify that \(\mathbb{E}[W_t^2]=t\).

Let \(f(x)=x^2\). Then \(f'(x)=2x\), \(f''(x)=2\). Here \(dW_t = 0\cdot dt + 1\cdot dW_t\) (drift 0, diffusion 1). By Ito's lemma:

$$ d(W_t^2) = \left(0 + 0 + \frac{1}{2}\cdot 2\right)dt + 2W_t\,dW_t = dt + 2W_t\,dW_t. $$

Integrating: \(W_t^2 = t + 2\int_0^t W_s\,dW_s\). Taking expectation and using \(\mathbb{E}[\int_0^t W_s\,dW_s]=0\) (Ito integral has zero mean), we get \(\mathbb{E}[W_t^2]=t\).

Exercise 2.3.2. Show that \(\mathbb{E}[S_t] = S_0 e^{\mu t}\) for geometric Brownian motion \(dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW\).

From the exact solution \(S_t = S_0\exp[(\mu-\sigma^2/2)t + \sigma W_t]\):

$$ \mathbb{E}[S_t] = S_0\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left((\mu-\sigma^2/2)t + \sigma W_t\right)\right]. $$

Since \(W_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,t)\), the moment-generating function gives \(\mathbb{E}[e^{\sigma W_t}] = e^{\sigma^2 t/2}\). Therefore:

$$ \mathbb{E}[S_t] = S_0\,e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)t}\cdot e^{\sigma^2 t/2} = S_0\,e^{\mu t}. $$

Exercise 2.3.3. Consider the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process \(dX_t = -\theta X_t\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t\) with \(\theta>0\). Use the integrating factor \(e^{\theta t}\) to find the exact solution and compute \(\mathbb{E}[X_t]\) and \(\text{Var}(X_t)\).

Let \(Y_t = e^{\theta t}X_t\). By Ito's lemma (with \(\mu=-\theta X_t\) and diffusion \(\sigma\), and \(f(x,t)=e^{\theta t}x\)):

$$ dY_t = \theta e^{\theta t}X_t\,dt + e^{\theta t}(-\theta X_t\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t) = \sigma e^{\theta t}\,dW_t. $$

Integrate: \(Y_t - Y_0 = \sigma\int_0^t e^{\theta s}\,dW_s\), so

$$ X_t = X_0 e^{-\theta t} + \sigma\int_0^t e^{-\theta(t-s)}\,dW_s. $$

\(\mathbb{E}[X_t] = X_0 e^{-\theta t}\) (the Ito integral has zero mean).

\(\text{Var}(X_t) = \sigma^2\int_0^t e^{-2\theta(t-s)}\,ds = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}(1-e^{-2\theta t}) \to \frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}\) as \(t\to\infty\).

Computational Exercises

Exercise 2.3.5. Implement the Euler-Maruyama method for the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process \(dX=-X\,dt+0.5\,dW\) starting at \(X_0=2\). Generate 50 paths and plot them. Overlay the theoretical mean \(\mathbb{E}[X_t]=2e^{-t}\) and \(\pm 2\) standard deviation bands.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

np.random.seed(0)
theta, sigma, X0, T = 1.0, 0.5, 2.0, 5.0
N, n_paths = 1000, 50
dt = T / N
t = np.linspace(0, T, N + 1)

paths = np.zeros((n_paths, N + 1))
paths[:, 0] = X0
for i in range(n_paths):
    for n in range(N):
        dW = np.sqrt(dt) * np.random.randn()
        paths[i, n+1] = paths[i, n] - theta * paths[i, n] * dt + sigma * dW

mean_t = X0 * np.exp(-theta * t)
var_t = (sigma**2 / (2*theta)) * (1 - np.exp(-2*theta*t))
std_t = np.sqrt(var_t)

plt.figure(figsize=(10, 5))
for i in range(n_paths):
    plt.plot(t, paths[i], alpha=0.3, linewidth=0.5)
plt.plot(t, mean_t, 'k-', linewidth=2, label='E[X(t)]')
plt.fill_between(t, mean_t - 2*std_t, mean_t + 2*std_t,
                 alpha=0.2, color='orange', label='2-sigma band')
plt.xlabel('t'); plt.ylabel('X(t)')
plt.title('Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Process: Euler-Maruyama')
plt.legend(); plt.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
